## The Listening Circuit

During the residency I propose to develop SYMBIONT from a solo instrument into a closed loop between two nervous systems — the performer's and the listener's — using the Institute's facilities to make audible something empirical aesthetics usually observes from the outside.

Empirical aesthetics asks how a body responds to music: which physiological changes accompany being moved, entrained, chilled, or bored. SYMBIONT already turns one such signal — an EEG — into sound. What I have never been able to do alone is close the loop: to let a listener's own physiological response feed back into the sound they are hearing, in real time, and to know with any rigour whether what results is coherent as an aesthetic experience or only as a technical one. At the Institute this becomes possible.

Concretely, I would like to:

— work with the Department of Music (Prof. Dr. Melanie Wald-Fuhrmann) and the Institute's in-house EEG and physiology labs to route validated measures (EEG, and where relevant heart rate, respiration, skin conductance) into braino, so that a listener's arousal or attention modulates the sound in the room;

— study *entrainment* as a compositional material — the tendency of physiological rhythms to synchronise with an external pulse. This is where I most hope to work alongside the Research Group *Neurocognition of Music and Language* (PD Dr. Daniela Sammler), whose research on neural entrainment meets my question exactly from the other side. I want to compose not fixed pieces but conditions under which a listener and the instrument fall into, and out of, synchrony, and to use the group's expertise to tell the difference between felt coupling and my own projection of it;

— test these études with listeners under conditions the Institute can actually measure, treating the experiments and the compositions as one activity rather than two.

The scientific interest cuts both ways. For my practice, the residency offers instruments and interlocutors precise enough to challenge assumptions I have taken on faith — that a plant's fluctuation "means" something musically, that biofeedback produces intimacy rather than noise. For the Institute, the work offers an unusual object: an aesthetic experience that is also its own measuring apparatus, where the sound is generated by the very signals a study would otherwise record in silence.

The final project would take the form of a performance-installation for a small audience — a room in which the sound is authored, moment to moment, by the physiology of the people inside it — with a short documentation of the method. I would bring to it the patience my theatre and dance work has taught me: this is slow listening, and it asks an audience to sit inside a process rather than watch a result.

I come to Frankfurt not to illustrate science with art, nor to decorate it, but to build one honest instrument in the space where the two kinds of listening meet.
